Polycrystals ​

The whole page as one script

Every block of this page, in order, is scripts/material_laws/12_polycrystals.jl. From a clone of the repository, julia --project=docs -e 'using Pkg; Pkg.instantiate()' once, then julia scripts/material_laws/12_polycrystals.jl, runs it end to end.

A metal is an aggregate of grains, each a single crystal that deforms by slip on its own slip systems, oriented at random or along a texture. Its response follows from the single-crystal law of every grain and from a rule saying how the grains share the macroscopic strain and stress. This page states such rules for an aggregate of many grains: the uniform strain of Taylor, and the interaction of each grain with a matrix standing for the others, elastic after Kröner, elastoplastic after Berveiller and Zaoui, and the aggregate itself, with its anisotropic tangent, after Hill. The uniform-field composites are the uniform rules for a few phases. The example below runs an aggregate.

The grains ​

Grain has the volume fraction and a law of CrystalViscoplasticity, whose cube axes are the vectors of its orientation: cubic elasticity, slip on the twelve systems of the FCC or the BCC lattice, a critical resolved shear stress and a viscous overstress. On system of the grain, of normal and direction rotated with it, the resolved shear stress is    . As the viscous overstress becomes small (the parameter of the law), a grain slips only on the systems whose resolved stress reaches .

The Taylor model ​

Every grain undergoes the macroscopic strain:

It is the uniform-strain composite of the grains. Since a grain then sees nothing of the others, each is integrated on its own: Polycrystal keeps the state of every grain in a vector and calls the local integration of each, which scales to hundreds of grains, where UniformStrain solves all the phases in one system of equations.

To follow a plastic strain imposed on it, a grain has to activate enough slip systems to produce any isochoric strain, five independent ones in general. In elasticity the Taylor model gives the Voigt average of the rotated stiffnesses; for cubic crystals and a random texture it is isotropic, of bulk modulus   and shear modulus   .

Grains in a matrix: Kröner ​

Under the Taylor model a grain cannot strain differently from its neighbors, whatever their resistance. Kröner (1961) lets each grain be a spherical inclusion in a homogeneous matrix that stands for all the others. Grains and matrix share the isotropic elasticity ; the grain carries its plastic strain and the matrix the average one,   . The difference of the two is an eigenstrain in an elastic medium, and Eshelby's solution for a sphere gives the strain of the grain,     . For deviatoric plastic strains, it is the interaction law

A grain that slips more than the average is unloaded by the matrix, at the rate of the elastic shear modulus. Plastic strains soon exceed the elastic ones by far, and so the internal stresses grow until every grain slips almost as the average: past the elastoplastic transition, Kröner's model comes back close to Taylor's.

A matrix that flows: Berveiller and Zaoui ​

The matrix stands for grains that flow too, and accommodates the misfit of a grain plastically as well. Berveiller and Zaoui (1978) give it an isotropic secant elastoplastic behavior: its plastic strain proportional to its stress deviator,   , so that its shear modulus falls to    while its bulk modulus stays . The aggregate as the matrix: Hill ​

Kröner's matrix stays elastic, and the matrix of Berveiller and Zaoui yields isotropically. Steady viscoplastic flow: the tangent model ​

At large strains the elasticity of the grains is negligible, and the grains can be taken rigid–viscoplastic: PowerLawCrystal slips on each system at the rate   , so that its strain rate is deviatoric and homogeneous of degree in its stress. Linearized at its current stress, a grain has the tangent compliance , times its secant one; the aggregate, homogeneous of degree as well, has a tangent viscosity  .

The tangent model of Molinari et al. (1987) lets every grain, a sphere, interact with a homogeneous matrix that behaves as the aggregate linearized at its current stress: through its tangent viscosity, since the stress around the grain departs from the average. In their computations, as here, the matrix is isotropic and incompressible, of viscosity , and Eshelby's solution for a sphere gives the interaction law

the stress deviators, the macroscopic strain rate. Averaged over the grains the law gives  : the closure on the stress is the whole self-consistency condition. flow_stress solves it by the trust-region method of SimpleNonlinearSolve on the six components of , each grain stress being the minimum of a strictly convex function (the dual potential of the grain plus the quadratic term of the interaction), found by Newton's method with Armijo's line search. Masson et al. (2000) show that the tangent model mixes the tangent interaction with a secant relation between the stress and the strain rate of each grain, which drives its estimate toward the uniform stress (Sachs) as grows; the example below shows the trend.

The anisotropic matrix of Lebensohn and Tomé ​

The aggregate of a textured or strongly nonlinear polycrystal is anisotropic, and so is the matrix a grain sees. Lebensohn and Tomé (1993) keep it so: the matrix is the aggregate itself, of secant compliance on the deviators,   , linearized at its tangent  . The interaction of a spherical grain with it goes through the Hill tensor of that incompressible anisotropic matrix:

the inverse on the deviators (deviatoric_pinv of TensND). With the secant compliance of each grain at its stress, the grain stress is   ,    , and the strain rates average to when   , their equation (25). AnisotropicTangentModel solves that equation for the 15 components of by the trust-region method of SimpleNonlinearSolve, starting from the isotropic matrix of TangentModel. The Hill tensor of the incompressible matrix is computed by quadrature with its exact Green kernel, the acoustic tensor inverted on the plane orthogonal to each direction, where the paper adds a small compressibility. For linear grains the model is the linear self-consistent scheme, which the tests check against the package's own scheme on grains made compressible, the gap closing as the inverse of the bulk stiffness.

What is not covered ​

  • The Sachs model, every grain at the macroscopic stress, is the uniform-stress composite of the grains, for a few of them. For an aggregate of nearly rigid–plastic grains it needs the stress of the step to stay below the flow stress of every grain while it is sought, which a strain-driven iteration does not ensure; it is not offered for large aggregates.

  • The β rule of Cailletaud and Pilvin (1994).

Example: a random FCC aggregate ​

This example pulls an aggregate of copper-like grains of random orientations, under the Taylor model, and recovers the Taylor factor of a random FCC texture.

The aggregate ​

julia
using MeanFieldHomogenization, TensND, Random, Plots, ForwardDiff

τ₀ = 10.0                                                   # critical resolved shear stress, MPa
grain(frame) = CrystalViscoplasticity(; C₁₁ = 168.4e3, C₁₂ = 121.4e3, C₄₄ = 75.4e3,
    K = 0.05, n = 10, τ₀, frame)
rng = MersenneTwister(2)
frames = [random_basis(rng) for _ in 1:60]
aggregate = Polycrystal(grain.(frames))

Inside the aggregate ​

The state of every grain is kept. The number of slip systems that slipped in each grain over the last step:

julia
active(s_old, s_new) = count(abs.(s_new.y[1:12] - s_old.y[1:12]) .> 1e-8)
counts = active.(grain_states(r.states[end - 1]), grain_states(r.states[end]))
histogram(counts; bins = 0.5:1:12.5, label = "", xlabel = "active slip systems in a grain",
    ylabel = "number of grains")

Most grains slip on six or eight systems, a few on four. A rigid–plastic grain following an imposed strain selects five independent systems; the viscous law of the grains makes no such selection, and spreads the slip over every system whose resolved stress reaches its critical value.

Grains in a matrix: Taylor, Kröner, Berveiller–Zaoui, Hill ​

The models of Kröner and of Berveiller and Zaoui assume grains of the isotropic elasticity of the matrix; the grains below have it (  ), with a slower viscous law than above. Eight grains keep the page light:

julia
E_y, ν = 120e3, 0.3                                                     # MPa
C₁₁, C₁₂ = E_y * (1 - ν) / ((1 + ν) * (1 - 2ν)), E_y * ν / ((1 + ν) * (1 - 2ν))
iso_grain(frame) = CrystalViscoplasticity(; C₁₁, C₁₂, C₄₄ = (C₁₁ - C₁₂) / 2, K = 1.0, n = 5, τ₀, frame)
rng = MersenneTwister(2)
few = [random_basis(rng) for _ in 1:8]
pull = LoadingPath(range(0, 1; length = 7); ε11 = t -> 3e-3 * t)
q = plot(; xlabel = "axial strain (%)", ylabel = "σ₁₁ / τ₀", legend = :bottomright)
for (model, label) in ((TaylorModel(), "Taylor"), (KronerModel(; E = E_y, ν), "Kröner"),
                       (BerveillerZaouiModel(; E = E_y, ν), "Berveiller–Zaoui"), (HillModel(), "Hill"))
    rm = point_response(Polycrystal(iso_grain.(few); model), pull)
    plot!(q, rm[:ε11] * 100, rm[:σ11] / τ₀; lw = 2, marker = :circle, label)
end
q

Kröner's matrix, elastic, opposes any misfit of a grain with the full elastic shear modulus: past the onset of slip it holds the grains almost to the average strain, and the response stays close to Taylor's. The elastoplastic matrix of Berveiller and Zaoui yields with the grains, lets them deviate, and lowers the flow stress of the aggregate. Hill's aggregate constrains the grains by its own tangent, which softens in the directions of slip: it gives the softest elastoplastic transition of the four, then hardens slightly above the isotropic secant matrix of Berveiller and Zaoui, well below Kröner's elastic constraint.

Steady flow: Taylor, tangent and Sachs ​

Rigid–viscoplastic grains of the same random texture, pulled at a constant strain rate, for exponents from linear viscosity to nearly rate-independent slip:

julia
crystals(n) = ViscoplasticPolycrystal([PowerLawCrystal(; γ̇₀ = 1e-3, τ₀, n, frame) for frame in frames])
D = Mandel{Float64}(1e-3, -5e-4, -5e-4, 0, 0, 0)                  # 1/s, isochoric tension
# Sachs: every grain at the uniaxial stress s whose average axial rate is D₁₁.
function sachs(p)
    rate(s) = sum(w * crystal_strain_rate(c, Mandel(2s / 3, -s / 3, -s / 3, zero(s), zero(s), zero(s)))[1] for (c, w) in zip(p.grains, p.weights)) - D[1]
    s = 10τ₀
    for _ in 1:100
        s -= rate(s) / ForwardDiff.derivative(rate, s)
    end
    return s
end
exponents = [1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 12, 20]
taylor = [von_mises(flow_stress(crystals(n), D; model = TaylorModel()).σ) for n in exponents]
tangent = [von_mises(flow_stress(crystals(n), D).σ) for n in exponents]
anisotropic = [von_mises(flow_stress(crystals(n), D; model = AnisotropicTangentModel()).σ) for n in exponents]
lower = [sachs(crystals(n)) for n in exponents]
plot(exponents, [taylor anisotropic tangent lower] / τ₀; lw = 2, marker = :circle, xscale = :log10,
    label = ["Taylor" "tangent, anisotropic matrix (Lebensohn and Tomé)" "tangent, isotropic matrix (Molinari et al.)" "Sachs"],
    xlabel = "exponent n",
    ylabel = "Σ_eq / τ₀ at D₁₁ = γ̇₀", legend = :topleft)

Under linear viscosity the estimates differ by the heterogeneity of the grains alone, and the two tangent models nearly coincide: the aggregate of 60 grains is nearly isotropic. As grows, the Taylor estimate rises toward the Taylor factor, while the tangent ones stay well below it: the tangent viscosity of the matrix, times smaller than its secant one, lets the grains deviate from the average strain rate. The isotropic matrix bends back toward the uniform stress; the anisotropic one, stiff along the directions where the aggregate flows least, keeps the grains closer to the average and gives a stiffer estimate.
