Aging viscoelasticity (ALV) ​

Before this page

Aging linear Volterra behavior (ALV), the theory of the Volterra operators and of the schemes used below, and Homogenization schemes, whose RVE this page reuses. A material that does not age may also take the shorter route of Non-aging viscoelasticity.

In aging linear viscoelasticity the stress at time depends on the whole strain history through a relaxation kernel , the stress at under a unit strain held from :

Aging means that depends on , the age at loading, and not only on  . A creep compliance, an aging conductivity or diffusivity are kernels of the same kind; conduction and diffusion (second-order kernels) are treated in section 6. Everything below runs as is; longer versions are in scripts/50_visco_law_basics.jl … scripts/59_alv_sensitivities.jl.

1. A law and an operator ​

Two objects describe a material:

ViscoLaw — the lawVolterraOperator — the operator
what it isthe kernel , a function of two timesthat kernel discretized on a time grid: the matrix of the integral above
time gridnone: it can be evaluated at any one TimeGrid, fixed once for all; R(t, t′) reads its kernel at any instants of the grid's span
you build itby writing a function of , or from the law libraryfrom a law and a grid, a constant tensor, or a matrix
you use it todescribe a phasecompute: compose, invert, apply to a history; it is what homogenize_alv returns

You describe a material by a law; the package discretizes it into an operator to compute. One goes from one to the other in a line:

julia
law = maxwell_iso(10.0, 6.0, 3.0, 1.0)             # a law: k, μ and their relaxation times
times = [0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0, 8.0]              # the instants of the computation
grid = TimeGrid(times)
R = VolterraOperator(law, grid)                     # the operator on that grid
law_back = operator_law(R)                          # the kernel again, known on the grid
R(3.0, 0.7) ≈ law(3.0, 0.7)                         # an operator is also a function of (t, t′)
true

A phase of homogenize_alv may be given either: a law, with the instants as times, or an operator, which carries its grid. The result is always an operator.

2. Writing a law ​

A ViscoLaw wraps a kernel function of and a mode, :relaxation for or :creep for the compliance (their names in conduction are :conductivity and :resistivity). The kernel returns:

  • a scalar (Float64, Complex{Float64}), for a one-dimensional problem;

  • a fourth-order tensor (TensISO{3}(3k, 2μ), TensTI{4}, TensOrtho) or its   Kelvin-Mandel matrix;

  • a second-order tensor (TensISO{3}(k)) or a   matrix, for conduction, diffusion or permittivity.

An elastic tensor is accepted wherever a law is: its kernel is   .

2.1 From the law library ​

A material that does not age is best taken from the law library: ViscoLaw builds its kernel   .

julia
m = iso_rheology(zener_maxwell(30.0, 20.0, 1.0), zener_maxwell(10.0, 8.0, 0.7))   # bulk, shear
law_lib = ViscoLaw(m)

The same model also drives the Laplace-Carson route of Non-aging viscoelasticity, so that the two routes compare the same material, which is what the three-route check relies on.

2.2 Ready-made isotropic laws ​

julia
law_max = maxwell_iso(5.0, 2.0, 1.0, 0.5)     # 3k e^{-t/1} 𝕁 + 2μ e^{-t/0.5} 𝕂: k, μ, then their times
law_kel = kelvin_iso(3.0, 1.0, [5.0], [2.0], [1.0], [0.5])   # creep: k₀, μ₀, then one Kelvin branch
law_el = heaviside_law(TensISO{3}(15.0, 4.0))  # elastic: C H(t - t′)

2.3 Written by hand, aging included ​

Any function of , the current time and the time of loading, is a law. Aging means that it depends on , not only on  . Here a solidifying material, whose active fraction grows with the age at loading:

julia
solidified(t′) = 0.3 * t′^4 / (1 + t′^4)        # active fraction at the time of loading
law_M = ViscoLaw(:relaxation) do t, t′
    f = solidified(t′)
    TensISO{3}(3 * (3.0 + 2.0 * f * exp(-(t - t′))), 2 * (1.0 + f * exp(-(t - t′) / 0.5)))
end

TensISO{3}(3k, 2μ) is the isotropic tensor    . A phase that forms during the history is loaded only by the strains that follow its formation, and a phase that dissolves stops carrying stress: precipitated and dissolved switch a relaxation kernel on at or off at , for any law, sampled ones included. A hydrating paste is built from them, one phase per step of its hydration (aging basic creep of concrete).

3. Operators and histories ​

An operator acts on a history, a function of one time, ; on the grid both have two faces:

objectas a functionon a time grid   
an operator, VolterraOperatorof two times, a matrix acting on the values of a history
a history, TimeHistoryof one time, its values

The time grid ​

Every operator and every history of one computation lives on the same instants. A TimeGrid holds them; it is built once, and every object built on it carries it:

julia
grid = TimeGrid([0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0, 8.0])
TimeGrid(6 instants, 0.0 … 8.0)

time_grid returns the grid of an operator, a history, or the result of a computation on them. Two objects on different grids are refused rather than interpolated onto one another.

Jumps: an instant given twice ​

A creep test applies a stress at the loading age and holds it: the stress jumps at . Between two instants of the grid a history is linear, so on a grid where appears once the stress rises over the interval before , and the material creeps during that rise. Giving twice makes the jump exact: the first node is the left limit , the second the right limit . A history or a law given as a function is evaluated just before on the first node, so a function that jumps at is written right-continuous, t ≥ t′ ? … : ….

Here a stress held from   on a material that creeps logarithmically. The strain is zero up to and follows the compliance from on:

julia
jump = TimeGrid([0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0, 8.0])      # 1.0 twice: a jump at t = 1
J_log(t, t′) = t < t′ ? 0.0 : 1 / 30 + log1p((t - t′) / 0.5) / 60
creep = VolterraOperator(J_log, jump)
σ = TimeHistory(t -> t ≥ 1.0 ? 1.0 : 0.0, jump)            # 0 at 1⁻, 1 at 1⁺
ε = creep * σ
held = [i ≤ 3 ? 0.0 : J_log(t, 1.0) for (i, t) in enumerate(jump)]   # nothing up to 1⁻
[ε[i][1] for i in 1:length(jump)] ≈ held
true

The same holds for a phase that appears at (precipitated) or disappears at (dissolved): with given twice, a strain applied before leaves the phase unstressed, and a strain jump at loads it whole. The exact weights are derived in the theory.

Building an operator ​

From a law, any function of . Here an aging Maxwell material whose bulk and shear moduli relax, the stiffness of a phase being     (TensISO{3}(3k, 2μ)):

julia
age(t′) = 1 - 0.5 * exp(-t′)          # the material stiffens with its age at loading
R = VolterraOperator(grid) do t, t′
    age(t′) * TensISO{3}(3 * 10.0 * exp(-(t - t′) / 3), 2 * 6.0 * exp(-(t - t′)))
end
VolterraOperator: isotropic, order 4, 6 instants, blocks of sizes (1, 1), Float64

From a constant tensor (an elastic phase, whose relaxation does not depend on time):

julia
E = VolterraOperator(TensISO{3}(3 * 30.0, 2 * 20.0), grid)

From the matrix of an operator already discretized on the grid (  at order 4, with   Kelvin-Mandel blocks), as one scale hands it to the next or as another code writes it. like = C stores it in the symmetry class and frame of the tensor C, after checking that it belongs to it:

julia
M = Matrix(R)                                  # 36 × 36
R2 = VolterraOperator(M, grid; like = TensISO{3}(1.0, 1.0))
R2 ≈ R
true

Computing as with elastic tensors ​

An operator computes as its elastic counterpart does, with the same symbols:

elasticaging viscoelastic
C₁ ⊡ C₂composition   (it does not commute: keep the order)
inv(C)Volterra inverse
C₁ + C₂, C₁ - C₂, c * C, C / csum, difference, product by a number
Ithe identity operator
C ⊡ εthe response to a history

At order 2 (conduction, diffusion, permittivity) ⋅ takes the place of ⊡.

The creep operator of a material is the Volterra inverse of its relaxation operator:

julia
J = inv(R)

The stress under a strain history, and the strain recovered from it:

julia
ε = TimeHistory(grid) do t
    1.0e-3 * TensND.Tens(Symmetric([1.0 0 0; 0 -0.3 0; 0 0 -0.3]))   # uniaxial, held from t = 0
end
σ = R ⊡ ε
inv(R) ⊡ σ ≈ ε
true

σ[i] is the stress at , as its six Kelvin-Mandel components. A dilute estimate written as on paper,         , is one line of Julia; homogenize_alv computes such estimates for every scheme.

An operator is its matrix

A VolterraOperator is an AbstractMatrix: the   matrix of the discretized integral, stored compactly. Its matrix product is the composition, its matrix inverse the Volterra inverse, so it goes wherever such a matrix goes. Matrix(R) writes it out in full.

The matrix is that of the trapezoidal rule of Sanahuja (2013): on the grid the integral becomes a block lower-triangular matrix of   blocks at order 4,   at order 2. trapezoidal_matrix writes it out for a law, and volterra_inverse inverts such a matrix by forward substitution; a VolterraOperator holds that matrix.

Symmetry classes: what is stored ​

When the values are isotropic, transversely isotropic about one axis, orthotropic or cubic in one frame, each of them is a few small blocks (TensND symmetry_blocks): two numbers for an isotropic tensor, a   matrix and two numbers for a transversely isotropic one. The operator then stores, for each block, one matrix over the instants, and computes on those, block by block:

classblocks storedstorage, relative to  
isotropic, 1/18
cubicthree scalars1/12
transversely isotropic, any axisa  , two scalars1/6
orthotropic, any framea  , three scalars1/3
none (dense)the  1

An isotropic product costs two   products where the dense one costs a   one: about a hundred times fewer operations.

julia
R
VolterraOperator: isotropic, order 4, 6 instants, blocks of sizes (1, 1), Float64

The class is found from the values of the law, and an isotropic operator combined with another joins its class. operator_value(R, i, j) is the value at the pair of instants as a tensor of the class; convert_class stores an operator in another class or dense (DenseClass{4}()).

A value of the operator is not a value of the kernel

The matrix holds the weights of the trapezoidal rule, which mix the kernel at neighboring instants. The kernel is R(t, t′), below.

Operators and histories as functions of time ​

An operator is also the function and a history the function ; both are called as such:

callbuilt from a functioncomputed (a sum, an inverse, homogenize_alv) or given by values
R(t, t′)the law itself, at any ; zero for  its kernel recovered on the grid, interpolated between the instants
h(t)the function itself, as componentsits values, interpolated linearly between the instants

The interpolation is the one the trapezoidal rule integrates exactly (derivation): linear between the instants, right-continuous at an instant given twice, and nothing outside . The value is a tensor of the class of R (a number for a scalar operator), and h(t) the Kelvin-Mandel components, as h[i].

julia
J = inv(R)                                   # a creep operator, computed: no law
J(3.0, 0.7)                                  # its kernel, between the instants

Finding the cell of is a binary search; a computed operator then recovers the two rows of its kernel that the cell needs, a cost proportional to the number of instants. For many evaluations, recover the whole kernel once:

julia
J_law = operator_law(J)                      # every sample, once
J_law(3.0, 0.7) ≈ J(3.0, 0.7)                # then a binary search and four samples
true

Both accept ForwardDiff duals, in the instants and in the parameters of a law. A law-backed operator also takes symbolic instants; an interpolated one refuses them, since finding the cell compares instants.

homogenize_alv computes in these classes whenever the phases allow it, and returns its result in the cheapest class that holds it; the costs are tabulated in Symmetry classes and structured storage.

4. Homogenizing ​

A phase property may be a law, an operator on the grid, or a Homogenized cell, homogenized on the same grid, as in elasticity. With operators, the grid is read on them:

julia
rve = RVE()
add_phase!(rve, :M, Ellipsoid(1.0), Dict(:C => R); fraction = :rest)
add_phase!(rve, :I, Ellipsoid(1.0), Dict(:C => E); fraction = 0.3)
Rhom = homogenize_alv(rve, MoriTanaka(:M), :C)
VolterraOperator: isotropic, order 4, 6 instants, blocks of sizes (1, 1), Float64

The result is a phase of the next scale as it is, on the same grid; or, without computing it first, the cell itself:

julia
concrete = RVE()
add_phase!(concrete, :paste, Ellipsoid(1.0), Dict(:C => Homogenized(rve, MoriTanaka(:M))); fraction = :rest)
add_phase!(concrete, :sand, Ellipsoid(1.0), Dict(:C => VolterraOperator(TensISO{3}(3 * 40.0, 2 * 30.0), grid)); fraction = 0.6)
homogenize_alv(concrete, MoriTanaka(:paste), :C)
VolterraOperator: isotropic, order 4, 6 instants, blocks of sizes (1, 1), Float64

With laws, the instants are given as times; here the aging matrix of §2.3 and aligned oblate spheroids:

julia
cell = RVE()
add_phase!(cell, :M, Ellipsoid(1.0), Dict(:C => law_M); fraction = :rest)
add_phase!(cell, :I, Ellipsoid(1.0, 1.0, 0.5), Dict(:C => TensISO{3}(60.0, 20.0)); fraction = 0.2)
C_eff = homogenize_alv(cell, MoriTanaka(), :C; times)
VolterraOperator: transversely isotropic, order 4, 6 instants, blocks of sizes (2, 1, 1), Float64

5. Cracks in ALV ​

5.1 Traction-free elliptic and ribbon cracks ​

A crack family enters with its density, in the matrix law:

julia
cracked = RVE()
add_phase!(cracked, :M, Ellipsoid(1.0), Dict(:C => law_M); fraction = :rest)
add_phase!(cracked, :F, PennyCrack(1.0), Dict(:C => law_M); density = 0.05, symmetrize = :iso)
homogenize_alv(cracked, MoriTanaka(), :C; times)
VolterraOperator: isotropic, order 4, 6 instants, blocks of sizes (1, 1), Float64

Elliptic cracks of any aspect ratio   (EllipticCrack(a, b), the penny included) and of any orientation (euler_angles) are accepted, in an isotropic matrix. Their COD coefficients are the elastic closed form with every quotient a Volterra inverse, in the order of the flat limit of a void ellipsoid (cod_kernel_alv); their compliance is stored in the class of the crack, transversely isotropic about the normal of a penny, orthotropic in the frame of an ellipse, so that an oriented family is not a dense operator:

julia
add_phase!(cracked, :E, EllipticCrack(1.0, 0.4; euler_angles = (0.4, 0.9, 0.2)),
           Dict(:C => law_M); density = 0.05)
homogenize_alv(cracked, MoriTanaka(), :C; times)
VolterraOperator: orthotropic, order 4, 6 instants, blocks of sizes (3, 1, 1, 1), Float64

The density is    , as in elasticity (why, and how to convert the densities of other sources). Checked against Echoes on aging matrices (tilted ellipses with dilute, Mori–Tanaka and self-consistent schemes, two families of different orientations) to : see scripts/bench_echoes/alv_harness.

Ribbon cracks (RibbonCrack(b), unbounded along their first axis, of half-width b) are accepted too, with the density    per unit area; their compliance is orthotropic in their frame. A ConductiveCrack opens as the penny it wraps. The formulas, and the limits that check them, are in Cracks of the theory:

5.2 Cracks with finite interface stiffness ​

For a flat crack carrying a spring-like interface stiffness with time-dependent normal Rn(t,t') and tangential Rt(t,t') aging kernels, attach the interface laws as :Rn / :Rt properties of the crack phase, scalar laws of stiffness per unit area:

julia
law_Rn = ViscoLaw((t, t′) -> 3.0 * exp(-(t - t′) / 4))   # normal stiffness, relaxing
law_Rt = heaviside_law(1.5)                             # tangential stiffness, constant
bonded = RVE()
add_phase!(bonded, :M, Ellipsoid(1.0), Dict(:C => law_M); fraction = :rest)
add_phase!(bonded, :F, PennyCrack(1.0), Dict(:C => law_M, :Rn => law_Rn, :Rt => law_Rt);
           density = 0.05, symmetrize = :iso)
homogenize_alv(bonded, MoriTanaka(), :C; times)
VolterraOperator: isotropic, order 4, 6 instants, blocks of sizes (1, 1), Float64

The scalar COD kernels (normal) and , (tangential) are post-corrected, Rn on the first and Rt on the other two: crack-face and interface compliances add up, as if the springs saw the average opening, at the cost of one extra scalar Volterra inverse per direction. This is an approximation, exact in its limits below but not derived from the integral equation of a crack with springs (Interfaces).

5.3 How each scheme takes the cracks ​

A crack family has no volume, which decides how each scheme takes it (Cracks in the schemes):

SchemeCrack treatment
Voigt, Reussignored (no volume)
Dilute, DiluteDualits contribution added to the stiffness or the compliance
Maxwell, PCWone more contribution, of weight one
MoriTanakathe Echoes form  : the crack also enters the average strain, against the matrix
SelfConsistent, AsymmetricSelfConsistentthe same form, against the running estimate
DifferentialSchemeits contribution against the running medium, at the rate of its density

The Echoes form is also that of the elastic Mori–Tanaka scheme with cracks: the crack term in the average strain is what keeps it from the spurious percolation of a purely additive form at moderate density. The schemes on the running estimate need an isotropic orientation average of each family (symmetrize = :iso), see section 7.

Scripts: scripts/57_aging_creep_cracks.jl (every crack-aware ALV scheme) and scripts/15_cracks_iso_interface.jl (elastic and conductive cracks with interfaces, without aging).

symmetrize is honored in both orders. The projection is applied to the dilute quantities (, ) block by block, with the projector of the right tensor order — 6×6 Mandel blocks for order 4, plain 2-tensor blocks for order 2. It is exact in both cases: the SO(3) average of a 2-tensor is its spherical part  , and the azimuthal average about keeps the axial component, the transverse mean and the axial antisymmetric part.

The bounds are shape-blind: Voigt and Reuss average the phase relaxation matrices directly, so symmetrize cannot change their result.

A crack family in a conductor is insulating: it adds the resistivity     , with the thermal COD of the elastic theory in which is the Volterra inverse of the conductivity kernel (the formulas). 7. The isotropic reference of the self-consistent and differential schemes ​

Every ALV Hill kernel — order 2 and order 4 alike — is built for an isotropic reference medium: that is the condition under which the time and space parts decouple and a closed form exists at all. This is not a restriction on the result, which is generally anisotropic; it is a restriction on what the kernel may be evaluated against.

That splits the schemes in two:

  • Voigt, Reuss, Dilute, DiluteDual, MoriTanaka, Maxwell, PCW evaluate the kernel against the matrix, which is fixed and isotropic. Any inclusion shape and orientation is fine.

  • SelfConsistent and DifferentialScheme evaluate it against their running estimate — the fixed point for the former, for the latter. An aligned non-spherical inclusion, or a crack (whose contribution is transversely isotropic in its own frame), drags that estimate out of the isotropic class, and the kernel would no longer be valid there.

Reference-updating ALV schemes need an isotropic running medium

With SelfConsistent or DifferentialScheme in ALV, every inclusion phase must keep the running estimate isotropic. Two ways to satisfy that: spherical inclusions with an isotropic phase law, or an isotropic orientation average, symmetrize = :iso, which is also what randomly oriented inclusions or cracks mean physically. A LayeredSphere, isotropic by construction, is always accepted.

An RVE that satisfies neither raises an explicit ArgumentError naming the offending phase, rather than silently reading iso parameters off a matrix that is no longer isotropic. A non-isotropic ALV matrix is refused for the same reason.

julia
iso_cell = RVE()          # randomly oriented cracks: the orientation average makes this legitimate
add_phase!(iso_cell, :M, Ellipsoid(1.0), Dict(:C => law_M); fraction = :rest)
add_phase!(iso_cell, :I, Ellipsoid(1.0), Dict(:C => TensISO{3}(60.0, 20.0)); fraction = 0.3)
add_phase!(iso_cell, :F, PennyCrack(1.0), Dict(:C => law_M); density = 0.1, symmetrize = :iso)
homogenize_alv(iso_cell, SelfConsistent(), :C; times)
VolterraOperator: isotropic, order 4, 6 instants, blocks of sizes (1, 1), Float64

At order 2, aligned spheroids need the same average:

julia
needles = RVE()

The elastic schemes have no such restriction

This is specific to ALV. In the elastic pipeline the Hill tensor is available for anisotropic references, so homogenize(rve, SelfConsistent()) and homogenize(rve, DifferentialScheme()) handle a running medium of any symmetry class — the differential scheme even tracks it, growing its ODE state from the iso to the TI, ortho or fully anisotropic layout as the phases require.

8. The differential scheme in ALV ​

DifferentialScheme is available in both tensor orders, with the same keywords as the elastic scheme — trajectory, nsteps, abstol / reltol, alg, and formulation = :stiffness | :compliance (the dual form integrates the creep function  ):

julia
homogenize_alv(iso_cell, DifferentialScheme(; formulation = :compliance), :C; times)
VolterraOperator: isotropic, order 4, 6 instants, blocks of sizes (1, 1), Float64

Supported inclusions: ellipsoids and spheroids, LayeredSphere, and crack families through their density, at both orders — subject to the isotropic-reference requirement of section 7. The self-consistent and the asymmetric self-consistent schemes accept the same inclusions at both orders.

9. Solving the self-consistent equation: Picard or Newton ​

SelfConsistent() solves its equation by Picard iteration from the matrix kernel, with the keywords abstol, reltol, maxiters, damping, verbose and select_best. SelfConsistent(algorithm = NewtonDefault()) marches through the instants instead, solving at each one a small system by Newton's method (how); its maxiters is a cap per instant, and the keywords of the Picard iteration alone are refused:

julia
picard = homogenize_alv(iso_cell, SelfConsistent(reltol = 1e-12), :C; times)
newton = homogenize_alv(iso_cell, SelfConsistent(algorithm = NewtonDefault(), reltol = 1e-12), :C; times)
Matrix(newton) ≈ Matrix(picard)
true

Both reach the same estimate; Newton converges in a few iterations per instant where the Picard iteration slows down near percolation. The march starts from the Picard estimate, loosely converged, because the equation may then have several roots and Newton's method from the matrix may reach a non-physical one; such a root, with a negative instantaneous modulus, is refused, and an instant that does not converge is reported by a warning. Another algorithm, or a seed (init), is refused rather than ignored.
