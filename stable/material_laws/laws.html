The laws shipped ​

Six laws come with MaterialLaws. Each is an instance of ResidualLaw or PotentialLaw, written in a few lines of library.jl, and each is tested against a closed-form response. This page gives, for every law, its equations, its parameters, and its behavior along a few loading paths.

The parameter values below are chosen for this page as plausible orders of magnitude for the material named in each section; they are illustrations, not calibrated data. Stresses and moduli are in MPa, times in seconds.

julia
using MeanFieldHomogenization, Plots

Linear elasticity ​

LinearElasticity takes any stiffness tensor (TensND, any symmetry, written in any basis) or, isotropic, Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio . It has no internal variable and its tangent is itself. In uniaxial stress the axial slope is and the lateral strain  , for an aluminum alloy here:

julia
alu = LinearElasticity(; E = 70e3, ν = 0.33)
tension = point_response(alu, LoadingPath(range(0, 1; length = 11); ε11 = t -> 2e-3 * t))
tension[:σ11][end] / tension[:ε11][end], -tension[:ε22][end] / tension[:ε11][end]
(70000.0, 0.33)

Power-law elasticity ​

The nonlinear elasticity of the deformation theory of plasticity: linear in the volume change, a power of exponent in the shear,

with   and the von Mises stress.   is linear elasticity of shear modulus ;   tends to rigid–perfect plasticity of yield stress . It is the law of the phases in the nonlinear composites of Ponte Castañeda (1991).

parametermeaning
kbulk modulus
σ₀, ε₀the equivalent stress reached at the equivalent strain
mthe exponent, between 0 (perfectly plastic) and 1 (linear)

An isochoric extension     , for which  , traces the law of the shear part:

julia
isochoric(e_max) = LoadingPath(range(0, 1; length = 41); ε11 = t -> e_max * t,
    ε22 = t -> -e_max * t / 2, ε33 = t -> -e_max * t / 2, ε23 = 0, ε13 = 0, ε12 = 0)
p = plot(; xlabel = "equivalent strain ε_eq (‰)", ylabel = "equivalent stress σ_eq (MPa)", legend = :topleft)
for m in (0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0)
    law = PowerLawElasticity(; k = 140e3, σ₀ = 60.0, ε₀ = 1e-3, m)
    r = point_response(law, isochoric(4e-3))
    plot!(p, r[:ε11] * 1e3, r[:σ11] - r[:σ22]; label = "m = $m", lw = 2)
end
p

Maxwell viscoelasticity ​

A spring of stiffness in series with a dashpot of viscosity  , written as two potentials of the viscous strain :

Every mode relaxes with the same time . A polymer binder, held at a fixed strain and pulled at two strain rates:

julia
binder = MaxwellViscoelasticity(; E = 1e3, ν = 0.35, τ = 30.0)
held = point_response(binder, LoadingPath(range(0, 120; length = 121); ε11 = 1e-3, ε22 = 0, ε33 = 0))
p1 = plot(held[:t], held[:σ11] / held[:σ11][1]; label = "computed", lw = 2,
    xlabel = "time (s)", ylabel = "σ₁₁ / σ₁₁(0)", title = "relaxation at fixed strain")
plot!(p1, held[:t], exp.(-held[:t] / 30); label = "exp(−t/τ)", ls = :dash)
p2 = plot(; xlabel = "axial strain ε₁₁ (‰)", ylabel = "axial stress σ₁₁ (MPa)", title = "constant strain rate", legend = :topleft)
for rate in (1e-5, 1e-4)
    T = 2e-3 / rate
    r = point_response(binder, LoadingPath(range(0, T; length = 81); ε11 = t -> rate * t))
    plot!(p2, r[:ε11] * 1e3, r[:σ11]; label = "ε̇ = $rate /s", lw = 2)
end
plot(p1, p2; layout = (1, 2), size = (900, 340))

The slower test leaves more time to relax, so the stress stays lower. In uniaxial stress it tends to the plateau    where the viscous flow takes the whole strain rate:     MPa for the slower one. At a rate fast compared with the response tends to the elastic one.

Biot poroelasticity ​

A law of two coupled fields: the strain and the pore pressure , conjugate to the stress and to the change of fluid content (Coussy (2004)):

parametermeaning
E, νthe drained elastic moduli
bBiot's coefficient, between the porosity and 1
MBiot's modulus

A sandstone compressed in an oedometer, drained (the pore pressure held at zero) and undrained (no fluid exchanged,  ):

julia
sandstone = BiotPoroelasticity(; E = 12e3, ν = 0.22, b = 0.75, M = 9e3)
oedo = (ε11 = t -> -2e-3 * t, ε22 = 0, ε33 = 0)
drained = point_response(sandstone, LoadingPath(range(0, 1; length = 21); oedo..., p = 0))
undrained = point_response(sandstone, LoadingPath(range(0, 1; length = 21); oedo...))
p1 = plot(-drained[:ε11] * 1e3, -drained[:σ11]; label = "drained", lw = 2,
    xlabel = "axial shortening (‰)", ylabel = "axial compression (MPa)", legend = :topleft)
plot!(p1, -undrained[:ε11] * 1e3, -undrained[:σ11]; label = "undrained", lw = 2)
p2 = plot(-undrained[:ε11] * 1e3, undrained[:p]; label = "undrained", lw = 2, color = 2,
    xlabel = "axial shortening (‰)", ylabel = "pore pressure p (MPa)", legend = :topleft)
plot(p1, p2; layout = (1, 2), size = (900, 340))

The trapped fluid takes part of the load as pore pressure, and stiffens the sample by .

Norton viscoplasticity ​

Isotropic elasticity with a power-law creep of the plastic strain:

integrated by the backward Euler scheme. Under a constant uniaxial stress the creep rate is : the exponent sets how fast it rises with the stress. A steel creeping at high temperature,  :

julia
hot_steel = NortonViscoplasticity(; E = 160e3, ν = 0.3, K = 3000.0, n = 6.0)
p1 = plot(; xlabel = "time (h)", ylabel = "axial strain ε₁₁ (%)", title = "creep at constant stress", legend = :topleft)
for σ in (150.0, 180.0, 210.0)
    r = point_response(hot_steel, LoadingPath(range(0, 3600 * 10; length = 21); σ11 = σ))
    plot!(p1, r[:t] / 3600, r[:ε11] * 100; label = "σ = $(round(Int, σ)) MPa", lw = 2)
end
held = point_response(hot_steel, LoadingPath(range(0, 3600 * 10; length = 101); ε11 = 2e-3, ε22 = 0, ε33 = 0))
p2 = plot(held[:t] / 3600, held[:σ11]; lw = 2, label = nothing,
    xlabel = "time (h)", ylabel = "axial stress σ₁₁ (MPa)", title = "relaxation at fixed strain")
plot(p1, p2; layout = (1, 2), size = (900, 340))

At constant stress the creep is steady, a straight line; at fixed strain the stress relaxes, fast at first and then ever slower, since the creep rate falls with the stress as its sixth power.

Von Mises plasticity ​

Rate-independent plasticity with the von Mises criterion, isotropic hardening and linear kinematic hardening:

being the cumulated plastic strain.     by default; any function of may be given instead (hardening = p -> …), here a saturating one. The integration over a step is the backward Euler scheme, which for linear hardening is the radial return (Simo and Hughes, 1998).

parametermeaning
E, νthe elastic moduli
σ₀the initial yield stress
Hthe linear isotropic hardening modulus
Hkinthe linear kinematic hardening modulus
hardeninga function   replacing  

A structural steel under one tension–compression cycle, with three hardenings of comparable strength:

julia
σ₀ = 275.0
steels = (
    "isotropic, linear" => J2Plasticity(; E = 210e3, ν = 0.3, σ₀, H = 5e3),
    "kinematic, linear" => J2Plasticity(; E = 210e3, ν = 0.3, σ₀, Hkin = 5e3),
    "isotropic, saturating" => J2Plasticity(; E = 210e3, ν = 0.3, σ₀, hardening = p -> σ₀ + 150 * (1 - exp(-60p))),
)
cycle(t) = 6e-3 * (t < 0.25 ? 4t : t < 0.75 ? 2 - 4t : 4t - 4)
p = plot(; xlabel = "axial strain ε₁₁ (‰)", ylabel = "axial stress σ₁₁ (MPa)", legend = :outerright, size = (760, 380))
for (name, law) in steels
    r = point_response(law, LoadingPath(range(0, 1; length = 161); ε11 = cycle))
    plot!(p, r[:ε11] * 1e3, r[:σ11]; label = name, lw = 2)
end
p

Isotropic hardening expands the elastic domain, so reverse yielding starts late; kinematic hardening moves it, so reverse yielding starts early (the Bauschinger effect); the saturating law hardens fast at first and then flattens to   MPa.

Single-crystal viscoplasticity ​

A crystal deforms by slip on the systems of its lattice: a plane of normal and a direction in it, of Schmid tensor   . Each system slips when its resolved shear stress exceeds its critical value, and hardens with the slip accumulated on all of them (Méric et al. (1991)):

parametermeaning
C₁₁, C₁₂, C₄₄the cubic elastic constants
lattice:fcc (12 systems ) or :bcc (12 systems ), see slip_systems
framethe orientation: a TensND basis whose vectors are the cube axes
K, nthe viscous stress scale and exponent of the slip rate
τ₀the initial critical resolved shear stress
Q, bthe saturation and the rate of the hardening
latentthe interaction between systems: 1, every system hardens every other alike

The response depends on the orientation of the crystal with respect to the load, through the Schmid factors    of the loading axis . A copper-like FCC crystal pulled at a constant strain rate along three of its directions, each brought onto the tensile axis by rotating the crystal:

julia
using TensND, StaticArrays, LinearAlgebra
# The crystal frame that brings the crystal direction d onto the global e₁.
function along(d)
    d = normalize(SVector{3, Float64}(d))
    u = normalize(cross(d, abs(d[3]) < 0.9 ? SVector(0.0, 0, 1) : SVector(0.0, 1, 0)))
    return RotatedBasis(SMatrix{3, 3}(vcat(d', u', cross(d, u)')))
end
crystal(frame) = CrystalViscoplasticity(; C₁₁ = 168e3, C₁₂ = 121e3, C₄₄ = 75e3, frame,
    K = 10.0, n = 10.0, τ₀ = 10.0, Q = 3.0, b = 20.0)
pull = LoadingPath(range(0, 20; length = 81); ε11 = t -> 1e-3 * t)       # 1e-3 /s up to 2 %
p = plot(; xlabel = "axial strain ε₁₁ (%)", ylabel = "axial stress σ₁₁ (MPa)", legend = :bottomright)
for (name, d) in ("[001]" => (0, 0, 1), "[011]" => (0, 1, 1), "[111]" => (1, 1, 1))
    r = point_response(crystal(along(d)), pull)
    plot!(p, r[:ε11] * 100, r[:σ11]; label = "tension along $name", lw = 2)
end
p

Along and the most stressed systems have the largest Schmid factor,  , and the crystal yields at the same axial stress; along it is , and the crystal yields about 1.5 times higher. The elastic slopes differ too: a cubic crystal is stiffer along than along .
