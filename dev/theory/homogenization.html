Homogenization schemes ​

The previous chapters solved the problem of a single heterogeneity in an infinite medium: an ellipsoid, a crack, a layered pattern. A homogenization scheme turns those solutions into an estimate of the effective property of a representative volume element, by stating which auxiliary problem each phase is assumed to solve. This page sets the framework that all the schemes share. The pages that follow treat them one at a time, each with its figure, its assumption and its equations.

1. The representative volume element ​

The representative volume element (RVE) holds

  • a matrix phase of property tensor (or for the order-2 conductivity problem), when one phase plays that role;

  • one or more inclusion phases of property tensors , geometries , and amounts (volume fraction) or (crack density).

That picture is the mean-field approximation, and every scheme of this chapter is one answer to the question it leaves open: what is , and in which medium is each inclusion embedded? Taking   in the matrix gives the dilute estimate; requiring the matrix average to come out right gives Mori–Tanaka; embedding each phase in the unknown effective medium gives the self-consistent scheme.

The pages of the chapter follow the order of the table. The bounds use no shape at all. The next four pages embed the inclusions in the matrix, each with a richer description of what surrounds them. The self-consistent and differential schemes then replace the matrix by the effective medium, the laminate is solved exactly, and the last two schemes resolve the interaction inclusion by inclusion. A last page, on Levin's theorem, applies to all of them.

4. Schemes that need positions ​

Every scheme of the table but the last two sees one inclusion in a reference medium and accounts for the others only through that reference: the interaction is treated in an average sense. The ClusterModel and the EquivalentInclusion method drop that one-site assumption and resolve the interaction inclusion by inclusion, which needs strictly more information than an RVE carries — the positions. They act on a ParticleAssembly instead, and share one ingredient, the two-inclusion interaction tensor . Both degenerate exactly onto Mori–Tanaka when the interaction is switched off, which is the sharpest available check that their assembly is right.

5. Number-type compatibility ​

The schemes involve nothing but tensor algebra and, for the iterative and differential ones, a solver, so that the scalar type is left open. Every scheme is mandated to support:

  • Float64 — default;

  • ForwardDiff.Dual — sensitivity analysis through fractions, moduli, geometric parameters;

  • Complex{Float64} — frequency-domain viscoelasticity (parity with the C++ ECHOES library, templated on T = double | complex<double>);

  • SymPy.Sym, Symbolics.Num, BigFloat — best-effort, with explicit documentation of any limitation (the iterative SC solvers are not symbolic-friendly because the linear-system Jacobian must be numeric).

Where to go next ​

The schemes are now taken one at a time, starting with the two that need neither a shape nor a reference medium: Voigt and Reuss bounds. The tutorials A first homogenization and Bounds and classical schemes apply the schemes of this chapter to a first RVE, and the manual page Homogenization schemes gives their syntax.
