Damage by the growth of microcracks ​

This example runs the damage law derived from the microcracks of a brittle solid. It compares a progressive damage with the unstable growth that Griffith's criterion gives, and it shows how a viscosity regularizes the latter.

The cracked solid ​

A rock-like solid (bulk modulus 30 GPa, shear modulus 20 GPa) contains penny-shaped microcracks of all orientations, of initial density  . The stiffness of the cracked solid is the Mori–Tanaka one, as for a linear computation:

julia
using MeanFieldHomogenization, TensND, Plots

k, μ = 30e3, 20e3                                                 # MPa
C₀ = TensISO{3}(3k, 2μ)
rve = RVE()
add_phase!(rve, :M, Ellipsoid(1.0), Dict(:C => C₀); fraction = :rest)
add_phase!(rve, :F, PennyCrack(1.0), Dict(:C => C₀); density = 0.05, symmetrize = :iso)

symmetrize = :iso spreads the family over every orientation, so that the solid stays isotropic as the cracks grow.

Progressive damage ​

A first resistance increases with the density, as if the cracks met obstacles while they grow:

julia
rising(d) = 0.04 * (1 + 20 * (d - 0.05))                          # MPa
progressive = CrackGrowthDamage(rve; resistance = rising)
codetheory
rvethe cracked solid; the density of the crack phase is the initial one
resistancethe resistance that the driving force must reach
CrackGrowthDamagethe law whose internal variable is the density of each family

The bar is pulled, unloaded to zero strain, then pulled further. Its lateral faces are free.

julia
ε_axial(t) = t < 0.5 ? 3e-3 * t / 0.5 : t < 0.7 ? 3e-3 * (0.7 - t) / 0.2 : 4e-3 * (t - 0.7) / 0.3
r = point_response(progressive, LoadingPath(range(0, 1; length = 61); ε11 = ε_axial))

p1 = plot(r[:ε11] * 1e3, r[:σ11]; lw = 2, label = "", xlabel = "axial strain (‰)", ylabel = "axial stress (MPa)")
p2 = plot(r[:ε11] * 1e3, r[:F]; lw = 2, label = "", xlabel = "axial strain (‰)", ylabel = "crack density d")
plot(p1, p2; layout = (1, 2), size = (760, 320), left_margin = 3Plots.mm, bottom_margin = 4Plots.mm)

The response is linear until the driving force reaches the resistance, near 1 ‰. The cracks then grow, and the stress bends under its linear trend. On unloading the density is kept: the stress returns to the origin along a straight line, whose slope is the stiffness of the more cracked solid. On reloading, the density grows again only past the largest strain reached before. Nothing in this response was fitted except the resistance. The loss of stiffness is what the homogenization gives for the density reached.

Unstable growth, regularized ​

Griffith's resistance, for cracks of initial radius 1 mm and a toughness of 20 J/m², decreases as the cracks grow:

julia
griffith = CrackGrowthResistance(; Gc = 0.02, a₀ = 1.0, d₀ = 0.05)   # N/mm, mm

The growth is then unstable: at the threshold the density jumps. A rate-independent law cannot follow the jump from the density of the last step, so the growth is regularized by a time , compared here with the duration of the loading (one unit of time):

julia
path = LoadingPath(range(0, 1; length = 41); ε11 = t -> 2e-3 * t)
p1 = plot(; xlabel = "axial strain (‰)", ylabel = "axial stress (MPa)", legend = :bottomright)
p2 = plot(; xlabel = "axial strain (‰)", ylabel = "crack density d", legend = :topleft)
for τ in (1.0, 0.1, 0.01)
    rτ = point_response(CrackGrowthDamage(rve; resistance = griffith, τ), path)
    plot!(p1, rτ[:ε11] * 1e3, rτ[:σ11]; lw = 2, label = "τ = $τ")
    plot!(p2, rτ[:ε11] * 1e3, rτ[:F]; lw = 2, label = "τ = $τ")
end
plot(p1, p2; layout = (1, 2), size = (760, 320), left_margin = 3Plots.mm, bottom_margin = 4Plots.mm)

With the largest the cracks grow slowly and the stress keeps rising for a while past the threshold. As decreases, the curves approach the rate-independent response: the density jumps at the threshold, and the stress drops at once to the value that the more cracked solid carries. The peak stress is the same for   and  , the threshold of the rate-independent law; the response after the peak still moves with , and a smaller needs shorter steps, which the driver takes by cutting those that fail.

Checks ​

The tangent is consistent. The contract checker compares the tangent from the implicit-function theorem with the derivative of the stress, on a damaging step:

julia
check_material_interface(progressive; verbose = false)
true

Below the threshold, the law is the linear cracked solid. Its tangent is then the Mori–Tanaka stiffness at the initial density:

julia
σ, 𝕂, _ = point_update(progressive, Mandel{Float64}(2e-4, -1e-4, 0, 1e-4, 0, 0), initial_state(progressive), 1.0)
𝕂 ≈ KM(homogenize(rve, MoriTanaka(), :C))
true

Going further ​

  • Oriented families (cracks with a given normal, without symmetrize) grow each under the tractions on their own plane, so that the damage becomes anisotropic. Each family may have its own resistance: resistance = (F1 = R₁, F2 = R₂).

  • The law is a law of MaterialPoints and runs at the quadrature points of a finite-element computation. A softening law localizes in a structure, and the finite-element response then depends on the mesh.
