Layered cylinder ​

Before this page

Layered sphere — bulk + shear recurrences and imperfect interfaces, whose method is followed here, and Concentration and contribution tensors, which defines the tensors a pattern without a Hill tensor supplies to the Homogenization schemes.

LayeredCylinder is an infinite fiber of circular section made of n coaxial layers, in an infinite matrix, under a remote uniform strain or temperature gradient. It is the cylindrical counterpart of the layered sphere, with one difference in the constituents: each layer, and the matrix, may be transversely isotropic about the fiber axis. This is what a unidirectional composite needs, since its effective medium is itself transversely isotropic; a self-consistent or a differential scheme on aligned fibers therefore stays within the model.

The solution of the coated fiber goes back to the composite-cylinder model of Hashin and Rosen (1964) and, for the transverse shear, to the three-phase model of Christensen and Lo (1979); Hervé and Zaoui (1995) gave it for any number of coatings by transfer matrices, Hashin (1979) treated anisotropic constituents. The derivation below is made with symbolic computations on Layered cylinder: the transfer relations, symbolically.

4. Chaining the layers ​

In a layer, the amplitudes give the state at any radius, and a state gives the amplitudes back. The recurrence starts in the core, where only the regular modes exist, with a unit amplitude; at each radius it reads the state, applies the jump of the interface, and extracts the amplitudes of the next layer. In the matrix, the remote loading fixes the amplitude of the growing mode, which normalizes the whole sequence. This is the transfer-matrix method of Hervé and Zaoui (1995), written on states.

Two problems need a second sequence. The axisymmetric one is solved for and for separately. The transverse shear has two regular modes in the core, and ; two sequences are run and combined so that the matrix carries  .

A layer is the difference of two discs. Read on the outer side of an interface, an average includes what the interface carries — the opening of a spring, which is a strain without stress, or the surface stress of a membrane — and on its inner side it does not. The keywords external and internal choose the side, with the meaning they have for the sphere (Averages and interface jumps).

The axial stress is not given by a traction on the lateral surface. It is summed over the layers,      in each, plus the axial line force of each membrane; likewise a conducting surface adds to the axial flux.

The strain concentration and the average stress per unit remote strain are transversely isotropic about the axis. They are not symmetric: maps an axial strain to an in-plane one and not the reverse. The contribution     is symmetric when the cylinder carries all of the perturbation, its outer interface included (external = true, what the schemes use).

6. The composite-cylinder model recovered ​

A fiber coated by a matrix, read from outside, answers three of the four loadings as a homogeneous fiber, whatever the medium around it: its moduli , , and are those of the composite-cylinder model (Hashin and Rosen, 1964), and satisfy Hill's universal relations (Hill, 1964). Its effective stiffness is  :

julia
c = 0.36                                        # fiber fraction
k_f, p_f = 30.0, 14.0
k_m, m_m, p_m = 4.0 + 1.5 / 3, 1.5, 1.5           # coating: k = κ + μ/3, m = p = μ
C_eq = B ⊡ inv(A)
C₁₁₁₁, C₁₁₂₂, _, _, C₂₃₂₃ = ti_constants(best_fit_ti(C_eq, ez))
k_HR = k_m + c / (1 / (k_f - k_m) + (1 - c) / (k_m + m_m))
p_HR = p_m * (p_f * (1 + c) + p_m * (1 - c)) / (p_f * (1 - c) + p_m * (1 + c))
((C₁₁₁₁ + C₁₁₂₂) / 2, k_HR), (C₂₃₂₃, p_HR)
((6.967741935483871, 6.967741935483871), (2.727272727272727, 2.727272727272727))

Under transverse shear this equivalence does not hold: the answer of the coated fiber depends on the surrounding medium, and the model only bounds the modulus . Taking the surrounding medium as the unknown effective one is the three-phase model (Christensen and Lo, 1979), a fixed point of the schemes rather than a property of the pattern.

The cylinder is also the limit of elongated inclusions of the package, which share none of its code: a homogeneous prolate ellipsoid tends to the one-layer cylinder, and a confocal layered spheroid of growing focal distance to the layered cylinder. An imperfect interface carries a length (, , ), so that a section answers according to its own radius: the spheroid then tends to the average of the cylinders of its sections, not to the cylinder of its largest radius. Both limits are checked by the test suite and drawn in Coated fibers: the layered cylinder at work.

7. In the schemes ​

A layered cylinder has no Hill tensor: it enters every scheme by its tensors , and , evaluated in the reference of the scheme. That reference must be isotropic or transversely isotropic about the fiber axis. In a self-consistent or a differential scheme it is the running estimate, which aligned fibers keep in that class; a matrix phase is then given the shape of a one-layer cylinder.

julia
rve = RVE()
add_phase!(rve, :matrix, LayeredCylinder((1.0,), (coating,)), Dict(:C => coating); fraction = :rest)
add_phase!(rve, :fiber, cyl, Dict(:C => fiber); fraction = 0.4)
C_sc = homogenize(rve, SelfConsistent(), :C)
ti_constants(best_fit_ti(C_sc, ez))             # (C₁₁₁₁, C₁₁₂₂, C₁₁₃₃, C₃₃₃₃, C₂₃₂₃)
(7.101501216848348, 3.497112177564304, 3.28210824607282, 26.37815585155511, 1.9035923156447754)

With an orientation distribution on the phase (symmetrize = :iso), the tensors are averaged over the orientations, as for any inclusion.

8. Conduction, and impermeable layers ​

The transverse problem is that of the antiplane shear, with in place of . A layer of zero conductivity is admissible at any rank. No flux crosses it: the layers it encloses are shielded and carry no transverse gradient, and its own field, the limit of a vanishing conductivity, vanishes on its inner face,    . Two adjacent impermeable layers leave the inner one undefined and are refused.

julia
K(t, a) = TensTI{2}(t, a, ez)                   # (transverse, axial)
pipe = LayeredCylinder((0.4, 0.7, 1.0), (K(5.0, 7.0), K(0.0, 0.0), K(1.0, 2.0)))
[TensND.get_data(gradient_gradient_concentration(pipe, K(2.0, 3.0); layer = j)) for j in 1:3]

9. Aging viscoelasticity ​

In aging linear viscoelasticity each modulus is a Volterra operator and each amplitude a history (Aging viscoelasticity (ALV)). The relations of sections 2 to 5 hold with the products read in the order the mechanics gives them: a modulus acts on the left of the strain it multiplies, a division by a modulus is a left division, and a normalization by the remote amplitude acts on the right. In particular is  ; the other order does not satisfy equilibrium (the symbolic page shows both). The package writes the radial problems once, for numbers and for operators, so the aging cylinder is the elastic one evaluated on Volterra matrices: gradient_gradient_concentration_alv and property_contribution_alv take a LayeredCylinder whose layers are elastic tensors or ViscoLaws, with the interfaces of section 3, which do not age.

The axis of the cylinder is any direction. A layer or a reference transversely isotropic about is written in the frame of the cylinder, , where it is transversely isotropic about the third vector: its Volterra operator keeps the same blocks in the basis adapted to its class (two   scalars and a   array of them), only the axis of the class changes. The radial problems, and the schemes of a cell of such cylinders, are solved in that frame; the result, read back in the class about , is the operator in the canonical frame. A frame does not depend on time, so neither move alters the order of the Volterra products.

The reference of the Hill kernels of the aging schemes is isotropic. A layered cylinder needs no Hill kernel, so a cell made only of coaxial layered cylinders is solved by the self-consistent, asymmetric self-consistent and differential schemes with a running estimate transversely isotropic about their common axis; among other shapes, a cylinder phase takes an isotropic orientation average.
